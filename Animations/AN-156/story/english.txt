The musician Ishaq of Mosul rode out of Baghdad at dawn to escape court duties. At midday he sheltered beside a house and saw a singer enter.

Two invited young men entered, and Ishaq slipped in behind them as if he belonged. He ate and listened to the singer playing her lute.

While Ishaq stepped away, the host learned no one knew him but let him stay. The singer then performed a song Ishaq himself had composed.

One guest called Ishaq an intruder and rebuked him. He stayed silent, then quietly retuned the lute while the others were occupied.

The singer recognized that a master had tuned her instrument. Ishaq admitted doing it, then played and sang so brilliantly that everyone was astonished.

Ishaq revealed his name and his standing at court. The guests apologized, expelled the rude man, and begged for more music.

The merchant offered Ishaq the singer and her belongings if he stayed for a month. Ishaq agreed; meanwhile the caliph searched for his missing musician.

When the caliph learned the story, he rewarded the merchant with one hundred thousand dirhams and the singer with fifty thousand. The tale reflects an era when singers could be bought and gifted.
