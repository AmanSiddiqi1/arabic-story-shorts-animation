A scholar passed a small school|and met a well-dressed teacher.|He tested him in language, grammar and poetry,|and was impressed by his answers.
The scholar visited repeatedly|and discovered more knowledge each time.|He had assumed that schoolteachers|seldom combined learning with sound judgment.
On a later visit the school was closed.|Neighbors said|the teacher was mourning someone,|so the scholar went to his house to offer sympathy.
The teacher sat looking miserable.|Asked whether his father, brother or relative had died,|he said no:|he was grieving for a woman he loved.
The scholar tried to console him,|but the teacher admitted he had never even seen the woman.|The visitor asked|how such love had begun.
The teacher had heard a passerby|sing admiringly of a woman named Umm Amr.|Assuming she must be extraordinarily beautiful,|he fell in love with her.
Two days later the same singer said|Umm Amr and her donkey had gone and not returned.|The teacher took this to mean she was dead,|although the song never said so.
The scholar departed astonished.|The teacher knew many disciplines,|yet had built both a romance and a period of mourning|upon two songs and an untested guess.
