Prince Ma’an bin Zaidah went out hunting with his men on a hot day. They went far into the desert, and their thirst grew severe. They searched their containers for water but did not find enough even for the prince to drink.

As they went on, three girls appeared carrying waterskins on their shoulders. Ma’an approached them and asked for a little water. They did not ask his name, but gave him water at once.

Ma’an drank until his thirst was gone, then he wanted to thank the girls for their kindness. He turned to his men and asked for money to give them, but the men had brought no money with them on the hunting trip.

The prince thought for a moment, then opened his quiver. The arrowheads were made of gold. He gave each girl ten arrows, so the three girls received thirty precious arrows instead of money.

The girls looked at the arrows in amazement. One of them said to the other two, “Only Ma’an bin Zaidah would do this, the man known for his generosity. Even the arrows he uses in war carry gold!”

The girls agreed that each would thank him with lines of poetry. The first recited verses describing a man whose golden arrows help those in need, even when he goes out to meet his enemies in battle.

Then the second recited verses saying the prince gives generously to friends and enemies alike, and his generosity does not stop in times of war. The third described how the gold could pay for a wounded person’s treatment or a dead person’s shroud.

Ma’an listened to the girls’ praise, after they had given him water when he was thirsty. They had recognized who he was from his gift, not his clothing or attendants. The golden arrows stayed with them, a memory of the meeting in the desert.
