Ala al-Din, chief of police in Kus,|was at home one evening when a well-dressed stranger arrived|with a servant carrying a heavy chest|and requested a private meeting.
The chief welcomed him.|The stranger confessed that he had robbed travelers|but claimed he now wished to repent and earn an honest living.
Pointing to the chest,|he claimed it held jewels and precious metal worth forty thousand dinars.|He opened it to reveal gleaming ornaments,|rings and sparkling stones.
He said he wanted none of the goods from his former life.|He offered the whole chest for a thousand honestly earned dinars|to start a lawful trade.
Pleased by the apparent repentance|and the apparent bargain,|the chief ordered his treasurer to bring a thousand dinars.|He paid the stranger and accepted the chest.
The stranger thanked him|and left with his servant under cover of night.|The chief kept the chest without testing its contents|and waited until morning for an expert.
The next day the chief summoned a master goldsmith.|After examining everything,|the goldsmith declared the metal was brass and tin|and the supposed gems were only glass.
Furious, the chief sent men after the stranger,|but he had vanished.|The supposed reformed robber|had fooled the very official charged with catching thieves.
